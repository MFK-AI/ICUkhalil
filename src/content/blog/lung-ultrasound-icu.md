<h2 id="key-points">Key points</h2>
<ul>
<li>The 2025 focused update of the international point-of-care lung ultrasound recommendations contains 83 statements for adult practice.<sup><a href="#r1">1</a>,<a href="#r8">8</a></sup></li>
<li>A separate ESICM and ESPNIC consensus now standardises quantitative lung ultrasound scoring in intensive care.<sup><a href="#r2">2</a></sup></li>
<li>In ventilated ICU patients, lung ultrasound outperformed bedside chest radiography against CT across the common pleuro-pulmonary conditions.<sup><a href="#r3">3</a></sup></li>
<li>Lung ultrasound scores are most useful for tracking change in the same patient over time. Fixed numerical cut-offs are not yet reliable.<sup><a href="#r1">1</a>,<a href="#r8">8</a></sup></li>
<li>Absence of B-lines suggests the lungs will tolerate fluid. It does not predict that the patient will respond to fluid.<sup><a href="#r1">1</a>,<a href="#r8">8</a></sup></li>
<li>Lung ultrasound complements other imaging. It does not replace CT when the question needs full anatomical detail.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 id="why">Why lung ultrasound matters in the ICU</h2>
<p>CT remains the reference standard for chest imaging in critical illness. It requires transport of an unstable patient and exposure to radiation. Portable chest radiography avoids transport, but in a supine ventilated patient it misses much of what matters at the bedside. Lung ultrasound is fast, repeatable, radiation-free and performed by the clinician who makes the decision.</p>
<p>Its standing has also changed formally. The 2024 global definition of ARDS accepts lung ultrasound as an imaging method for bilateral opacities, especially where radiography is not available.<sup><a href="#r5">5</a></sup></p>

<h2 id="new">What the new recommendations say</h2>
<h3>The 2025 focused update</h3>
<p>The first international recommendations were published in 2012.<sup><a href="#r7">7</a></sup> The 2025 focused update, published in Intensive Care Medicine in 2026, used a Delphi process and produced 83 statements grouped into seven domains.<sup><a href="#r1">1</a>,<a href="#r8">8</a></sup> They cover the elementary signs, pneumothorax, interstitial syndrome, consolidation, pleural effusion, monitoring and prognosis.<sup><a href="#r8">8</a></sup> The scope is adult bedside practice. Training, procedures, paediatrics and artificial intelligence are outside it.<sup><a href="#r8">8</a></sup></p>
<p>Several statements change how findings should be read:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Normal pattern.</strong> Lung sliding with A-lines shows preserved aeration near the pleura. It does not prove the deeper lung is normal.<sup><a href="#r8">8</a></sup></li>
<li><strong>Pneumothorax.</strong> Diagnosis combines absent movement signs with absent aeration signs. In a supine patient, start in the least dependent areas and move laterally to look for a lung point. A visible lung point confirms pneumothorax with high certainty. In extreme instability, strongly suggestive findings can support immediate drainage.<sup><a href="#r8">8</a></sup></li>
<li><strong>Pleural effusion.</strong> Ultrasound separates fluid from consolidation and detects significant effusion more accurately than supine radiography.<sup><a href="#r8">8</a></sup></li>
<li><strong>Fluids.</strong> Absence of B-lines indicates tolerance to fluid, not fluid responsiveness. New B-lines during a fluid challenge may signal developing pulmonary oedema.<sup><a href="#r8">8</a></sup></li>
<li><strong>Monitoring.</strong> Semi-quantitative scores are most reliable for tracking change over time. Thresholds for prognosis, subtypes and treatment response remain variable.<sup><a href="#r8">8</a></sup></li>
</ul>
<h3>The quantitative lung ultrasound consensus</h3>
<p>ESICM and the European Society of Paediatric and Neonatal Intensive Care endorsed an international consensus on the technique and clinical use of quantitative lung ultrasound in adults, children and neonates. The panel included 20 intensive care experts and two non-voting methodologists.<sup><a href="#r2">2</a></sup> It gives practical guidance on performing a lung aeration score, which is most relevant to patients on ventilatory support.<sup><a href="#r2">2</a></sup></p>

<h2 id="accuracy">How accurate is it against CT?</h2>
<h3>Mechanically ventilated patients</h3>
<p>In a prospective single-centre study of 110 mechanically ventilated adults, each patient had a portable chest radiograph and lung ultrasound before CT of the thorax, which served as the reference.<sup><a href="#r3">3</a></sup> Lung ultrasound showed higher diagnostic accuracy than chest radiography for all major pulmonary conditions studied, including consolidation, pleural effusion, pneumothorax and pulmonary oedema.<sup><a href="#r3">3</a></sup> For pneumothorax, lung ultrasound reached 100% sensitivity and 97% specificity, against 66.7% sensitivity for chest radiography.<sup><a href="#r3">3</a></sup> The authors noted the single-centre design and operator dependence as limitations.<sup><a href="#r3">3</a></sup></p>
<h3>Pneumonia</h3>
<p>A 2025 Bayesian network meta-analysis pooled 9 studies and 746 ICU patients with CT as the reference. Lung ultrasound had a sensitivity of 0.93 and specificity of 0.83 for pneumonia. Chest radiography had a sensitivity of 0.65 and specificity of 0.81.<sup><a href="#r4">4</a></sup> The authors cautioned that moderate specificity, operator dependence and risk of bias limit these results, and that trials are needed to show whether wider use improves antibiotic stewardship or outcomes.<sup><a href="#r4">4</a></sup></p>
<div class="callout"><strong>At the bedside</strong>Lung ultrasound finds pathology that bedside radiography misses. Its weaker point is specificity for pneumonia, so interpret consolidation alongside the clinical picture, secretions and microbiology.</div>

<h2 id="practice">Using it in daily practice</h2>
<div class="callout"><strong>Author's synthesis</strong>The scanning approach below reflects common ICU practice and the documents above. Adapt it to local protocols and training.</div>
<h3>A standard examination</h3>
<p>The widely used aeration score examines six regions on each side of the chest: anterior, lateral and posterior, each divided into upper and lower zones. Each region scores from 0 to 3:</p>
<div class="tablewrap"><table>
<thead><tr><th>Score</th><th>Pattern</th><th>Meaning</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>0</td><td>Lung sliding with A-lines, or fewer than three isolated B-lines</td><td>Aeration preserved near the pleura</td></tr>
<tr><td>1</td><td>Three or more well-separated B-lines</td><td>Moderate loss of aeration</td></tr>
<tr><td>2</td><td>Coalescent B-lines</td><td>Severe loss of aeration</td></tr>
<tr><td>3</td><td>Tissue-like pattern of consolidation</td><td>Complete loss of aeration</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table></div>
<p>The total ranges from 0 to 36. Record it with the patient's position, ventilator settings and time, so that the next examination is comparable.</p>
<h3>Questions lung ultrasound answers well</h3>
<ol>
<li>Is there a pneumothorax after central line insertion, a procedure or a sudden rise in airway pressure?</li>
<li>Is there a pleural effusion large enough to drain, and where is the safest site?</li>
<li>Is the new opacity consolidation, effusion or both?</li>
<li>Will the lungs tolerate more fluid, and has a fluid challenge caused new B-lines?</li>
<li>Is aeration improving or worsening with treatment, prone positioning or PEEP changes?</li>
</ol>
<h3>Limits to respect</h3>
<ul>
<li>Ultrasound sees the pleura and the lung that touches it. Deep lesions surrounded by aerated lung remain invisible.</li>
<li>Subcutaneous emphysema, dressings, drains and obesity reduce image quality.</li>
<li>Findings depend on the operator. Training and quality review matter.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="training">Training and competence</h2>
<p>ESICM defined the core critical care ultrasound competencies expected of an intensivist. The list includes 15 echocardiographic, 5 thoracic and 4 abdominal competencies, plus diagnosis of deep vein thrombosis and ultrasound guidance for central venous access.<sup><a href="#r6">6</a></sup> The panel advised that skills beyond the core be developed in a structured way.<sup><a href="#r6">6</a></sup> Supervised scanning with CT correlation remains the fastest way to build reliable pattern recognition.</p>

<h2 id="uncertain">What remains uncertain</h2>
<ul>
<li>Whether lung ultrasound-guided management improves patient outcomes, rather than only diagnostic accuracy.<sup><a href="#r4">4</a></sup></li>
<li>Validated score thresholds for prognosis, ARDS subtypes and response to PEEP or prone positioning.<sup><a href="#r1">1</a>,<a href="#r8">8</a></sup></li>
<li>How best to standardise training and certification across countries.</li>
</ul>
