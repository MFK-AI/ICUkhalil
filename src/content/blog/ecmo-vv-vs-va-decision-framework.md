<div class="keypoints">
<h2 id="key-points">Key points</h2>
<ul>
<li>VV-ECMO supports gas exchange only. It needs a heart that can pump. VA-ECMO supports both circulation and gas exchange.</li>
<li>ELSO advises considering VV-ECMO for PaO2/FiO2 below 80 mmHg, or pH below 7.25 with PaCO2 of 60 mmHg or more, after optimal care including a prone trial.<sup><a href="#r1">1</a></sup></li>
<li>Randomised evidence supports VV-ECMO in selected severe ARDS.<sup><a href="#r3">3</a>,<a href="#r4">4</a></sup></li>
<li>Routine early VA-ECMO did not reduce 30-day mortality in infarct-related cardiogenic shock in ECLS-SHOCK, and it caused more bleeding.<sup><a href="#r6">6</a></sup></li>
<li>Echocardiography before cannulation decides most cases. The right ventricle is the organ most often missed.</li>
<li>Plan the exit strategy before you cannulate: recovery, transplant, durable device or a decision point.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 id="principle">The governing principle</h2>
<p>Venovenous ECMO drains venous blood, oxygenates it and returns it to the venous side. The patient's own heart still has to deliver that blood to the tissues. Venoarterial ECMO returns blood to the arterial side, so the circuit takes over part of the cardiac output as well as gas exchange.</p>
<p>The choice therefore rests on one question: is the lung failing, the heart failing, or both? Every step of the framework below is a way of answering that question with evidence rather than impression.</p>

<h2 id="vv">When VV-ECMO is the answer</h2>
<h3>Indications</h3>
<p>The 2021 ELSO guideline recommends considering VV-ECMO in severe ARDS with refractory hypoxaemia, defined as PaO2/FiO2 below 80 mmHg, or in severe hypercapnic respiratory failure, defined as pH below 7.25 with PaCO2 of 60 mmHg or more. Both thresholds apply only after optimal conventional management, including a trial of prone positioning when it is not contraindicated.<sup><a href="#r1">1</a></sup></p>
<p>Trials have used different entry points, including PaO2/FiO2 below 80 mmHg in EOLIA and a Murray score above 3 in CESAR. ELSO notes there is no strong evidence that either threshold is superior.<sup><a href="#r1">1</a></sup></p>
<h3>Evidence</h3>
<p>EOLIA defined its entry criteria as PaO2/FiO2 below 50 mmHg for more than 3 hours, below 80 mmHg for more than 6 hours, or pH below 7.25 with PaCO2 of 60 mmHg or more for more than 6 hours, despite optimised ventilation.<sup><a href="#r3">3</a>,<a href="#r5">5</a></sup> A meta-analysis of CESAR and EOLIA, used by the ESICM panel, found lower 60-day mortality with ECMO, with a risk ratio of 0.72.<sup><a href="#r3">3</a></sup> ATS suggests VV-ECMO in selected patients with severe ARDS, as a conditional recommendation with low certainty.<sup><a href="#r4">4</a></sup></p>
<h3>Relative contraindications</h3>
<p>ELSO lists older age and more than 7 days of mechanical ventilation at high settings, with plateau pressure above 30 cmH2O and FiO2 above 90%, among the factors that weigh against VV-ECMO.<sup><a href="#r1">1</a></sup> Both reflect a lower chance of lung recovery rather than an absolute bar.</p>

<h2 id="va">When VA-ECMO is the answer</h2>
<h3>Indications</h3>
<p>The ELSO adult cardiac guideline describes cardiogenic shock suitable for ECMO as systolic pressure below 90 mmHg, urine output below 30 mL/hour, lactate above 2 mmol/L and mixed venous saturation below 60%, despite treatment.<sup><a href="#r2">2</a></sup> Typical causes include acute myocardial infarction, fulminant myocarditis, cardiomyopathy, massive pulmonary embolism, refractory instability in hypothermia, and post-cardiotomy or post-transplant shock.<sup><a href="#r2">2</a></sup></p>
<h3>Evidence</h3>
<p>The evidence for VA-ECMO is weaker than many clinicians assume. ECLS-SHOCK randomised 420 patients with infarct-related cardiogenic shock and planned revascularisation. Thirty-day mortality was 47.8% with early ECLS and 49.0% with medical therapy alone, with no significant difference.<sup><a href="#r6">6</a></sup> Moderate or severe bleeding and vascular complications were more frequent with ECLS.<sup><a href="#r6">6</a></sup></p>
<p>About 77% of ECLS-SHOCK patients had cardiopulmonary resuscitation before enrolment, which led some to argue the case mix hid a benefit. A 2026 subanalysis found the effect of VA-ECMO stayed neutral across the whole range of resuscitation rates.<sup><a href="#r11">11</a></sup></p>
<p>In contrast, the DanGer Shock trial showed a mortality benefit with a microaxial flow pump in infarct-related shock, in a population that excluded comatose resuscitated patients.<sup><a href="#r9">9</a>,<a href="#r11">11</a></sup> The 2025 ACC/AHA acute coronary syndrome guideline upgraded microaxial pumps on that evidence and downgraded VA-ECMO in line with ECLS-SHOCK.<sup><a href="#r7">7</a>,<a href="#r8">8</a></sup></p>
<div class="callout"><strong>At the bedside</strong>VA-ECMO is not a default for every patient in cardiogenic shock. It remains a rescue tool for selected patients with a reversible cause or a clear bridge, chosen by a team that has considered the alternatives.</div>

<h2 id="grey-zone">The grey zone: mixed cardiorespiratory failure</h2>
<div class="callout"><strong>Author's synthesis</strong>This section draws on physiology and expert practice. High-quality comparative trials do not exist for these scenarios.</div>
<h3>ARDS with right ventricular failure</h3>
<p>Severe ARDS often strains the right ventricle through hypoxic vasoconstriction, hypercapnia, acidosis and high airway pressure. VV-ECMO can relieve much of this load by correcting gas exchange and allowing gentler ventilation. If the patient still perfuses, starting with VV support and reassessing the right ventricle within hours is reasonable. If shock persists, add an arterial return limb or convert to VA support.</p>
<h3>Differential hypoxaemia on peripheral VA-ECMO</h3>
<p>With femoral VA-ECMO, oxygenated circuit blood flows up the aorta and meets blood ejected by the recovering heart. If the lungs are still poor, the heart ejects deoxygenated blood into the coronary and cerebral circulation. Monitor a right radial arterial gas or a right-hand pulse oximeter, not only the left side. Options include optimising ventilation, adjusting circuit flow, or adding a venous return cannula to create a VAV configuration.</p>
<h3>Left ventricular distension on VA-ECMO</h3>
<p>VA flow increases left ventricular afterload. Watch for loss of arterial pulsatility, a closed aortic valve and pulmonary oedema on echocardiography and lung ultrasound. Unloading options include reducing flow, inotropes, an intra-aortic balloon pump, a microaxial pump or a surgical vent.</p>

<h2 id="framework">A bedside decision framework</h2>
<div class="tablewrap"><table>
<thead><tr><th>Bedside finding</th><th>Failing organ</th><th>Configuration to consider</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Refractory hypoxaemia or hypercapnia, adequate cardiac function on echocardiography</td><td>Lungs</td><td>VV-ECMO</td></tr>
<tr><td>Refractory cardiogenic shock, acceptable gas exchange</td><td>Heart</td><td>VA-ECMO, after considering other mechanical support</td></tr>
<tr><td>Refractory shock with severe respiratory failure</td><td>Heart and lungs</td><td>VA-ECMO with a plan for differential hypoxaemia, or VAV-ECMO</td></tr>
<tr><td>Severe ARDS with right ventricular strain, still perfusing</td><td>Lungs, with secondary right heart strain</td><td>VV-ECMO first, reassess the right ventricle early, escalate if shock persists</td></tr>
<tr><td>Cardiac arrest refractory to conventional resuscitation</td><td>Circulation</td><td>VA-ECMO as extracorporeal CPR, only within an established programme and in selected patients</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table></div>
<ol>
<li><strong>Confirm the indication.</strong> Is the cause reversible, or is there a bridge to transplant, a durable device or a decision?</li>
<li><strong>Exclude contraindications.</strong> Consider irreversible neurological injury, end-stage disease without a bridge, and prolonged high-pressure ventilation.</li>
<li><strong>Image before you cannulate.</strong> Assess left and right ventricular function, valves and the pericardium with echocardiography.</li>
<li><strong>Answer the organ question.</strong> Use the table above.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm conventional care is optimised.</strong> For the lungs this includes prone positioning. For the heart this includes revascularisation and appropriate drugs or devices.</li>
<li><strong>Involve the team early.</strong> The 2026 ESC guidelines give a class I recommendation for a multidisciplinary shock team when temporary mechanical support is considered.<sup><a href="#r10">10</a></sup> ELSO advises early transfer when the patient will need an ECMO centre.<sup><a href="#r1">1</a></sup></li>
<li><strong>Plan the exit.</strong> Write down the goal of support and the review points before cannulation.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="uncertain">What remains uncertain</h2>
<ul>
<li>Which patients in cardiogenic shock benefit from VA-ECMO. ECLS-SHOCK showed no benefit from routine early use, but did not identify a group that benefits.<sup><a href="#r6">6</a></sup></li>
<li>The best threshold for starting VV-ECMO. No single criterion has proved superior.<sup><a href="#r1">1</a></sup></li>
<li>The role of routine left ventricular unloading on VA-ECMO, which is under active study.<sup><a href="#r8">8</a></sup></li>
</ul>
